Slay the Spire · Ironclad · Act 1 · Ascension 0

An Ironclad that thinks two turns ahead

sts-for-ai is a home-built Slay the Spire combat engine and an AI that plays it. One hobbyist started it on 23 October; for most of the 23 days since, AI roles they set up ran the research, with the owner steering from time to time. This page is its history: everything that was tried, how the work was done, and how well that worked. Today it beats the Act 1 boss in about four runs of five on our test, and it is playing the real game.

Behind the scenes

How the work was done

A one-person hobby project, set up so the research could keep going while its owner was away. The way of working changed almost as often as the tests did. Here is how it grew, step by step.

  1. 23 Oct
    Spec first. The owner writes what they want, Claude writes a spec, the owner confirms it, and Claude builds it in steps that each end in a check a program can run. Every design call goes into a decisions log.
  2. 25 Oct
    Overnight sweeps. Training recipes run in batches at night, some on Google Colab; the morning is for reading results.
  3. 29 Oct
    A manager for the time away. One Claude session keeps a manager file with its state and decides on the owner's behalf, with standing instructions from the owner: decide, record why and the alternatives, prefer what can be undone, and keep work moving without waiting on the owner.
  4. 30 Oct
    A job queue and big-picture reviews. Experiments go into a queue that runs them unattended. After the owner asked for faster progress and more data analysis, a saved review workflow was set up to run about once a day and after any major result: several analyst agents read all the data through different lenses, a synthesis follows, and skeptic agents check the key claims. 13 such reviews ran.
  5. 2 Nov
    A fact store, a product registry, a training rule. Facts go into a store of sealed claims that cite their source files and turn "suspect" when a source changes; the best player lives in one registry file; training runs only when the net has something new to learn.
  6. 3 Nov
    Roles and lanes. The manager is split into scheduled roles (below). Research runs in lanes: short projects with a question, a predicted chance of success, a CPU-hour cap, an end date and a written "reopen if". A dashboard shows the machine, the queue and every session.
  7. 5 Nov
    One turn deep for comparisons. The owner's choice: every change is judged one turn deep, where a read costs minutes; the two-turn product is the reported score, not the ruler.
  8. 7 Nov
    Reads without a session. A family of queued jobs can run from one session to its verdict, so a lane needs no worker checking in between jobs.
  9. 9 Nov
    The owner works on Act 1 content directly. A lane spent about 3.5 hours of spec, review and speed checks on two cards, so the owner moved Act 1 content into their own sessions.
  10. 13 Nov
    Curator, "level is a pass". A daily curator role keeps the fact store current. A change that is more correct or more general passes when it reads level, without a gain.

Did the AI-run research work?

Fast and honest at trying things; slow to move the headline

Every lane wrote down, before it ran, how likely it thought success was and how much CPU it would need. Those records make it possible to grade the setup.

55lanes closed in 11 days
32of them closed the day they opened
25worked or built what they set out to (23 + 2)
21didn't work, each with a "reopen if"
~30%of the machine's thread-hours used by queued work

Were its odds honest?

Lane outcomes by the chance each gave itself beforehand

Yes. None of the ten long shots worked, about half of the even bets did, and most of the likely ones did.

Were its CPU estimates right?

35 lanes that gave an estimate

Most came within a factor of three either way. Seven used under a third of their estimate, mostly lanes that stopped early; the big training lanes ran 1.5 to 2.7 times over.

Was the machine busy?

Thread-hours of queued work per day, by the day each job ended

No. The rules said machine time was the bottleneck, but queued work filled about 30% of the dev machine's threads. The rest went idle while lanes wrote specs and code, waited for review, or waited for memory.

What worked

  • Speed of trying. 55 lanes closed in 11 days, most within a day, each with a question, a cap, a prediction and a written verdict.
  • Honest records. Failures were written up as carefully as wins, with what would justify reopening them. This page's "everything tried" list exists because of that.
  • It caught its own mistakes. The night of 28 October's passes were shown to be seed luck the next day; a boss cost on the mutated-monster test turned out to rest on one lucky control and was withdrawn by a read five times larger.
  • Goal 1 moved. The owner weighted unseen content at 0.8 of development. On it the lanes went from "new mechanics cost the net" to nets that remove about three quarters of the cost of mechanics they never saw.
  • Engine work landed fast: content as data files, a smaller game state, all 25 Act 1 monsters and all 75 Ironclad cards by 14 November.

What didn't

  • The headline didn't move. The product stayed the same from 3 to 13 November; about 865 of 1,550 booked thread-hours ran after 3 November without a change to it. Every gain on the Act 1 test came from search and card picks, and most gains found one turn deep shrank to about a third of their size two turns deep.
  • The machine sat idle. About 70% of its thread-hours went unused, while the rules called machine time the bottleneck.
  • Tests reversed each other. The recipe that ranked first on one test ranked fourth on the gauntlet; each ruler left out a part of what matters, so rulers kept changing.
  • Coordination cost. A lane needed hours of process for two cards, so the owner did that work in their own sessions.
  • Facts go stale. One rules change on 13 November flagged 31 stored facts for re-checking.
Results in brief

Where it stands, and six things it found

The headline number comes from one fixed test, the gauntlet: Act 1 boiled down to its fights. Here is the test, where the AI's runs end on it, and the findings players argue about, each with the measurement behind it.

  • Starter deck, 80 HP. Five Strikes, four Defends, Bash. Burning Blood heals 6 after every won fight. No other relics, no potions.
  • Eleven fights in a row. Three easy hallways, two hard ones, an elite, two hard, an elite, one hard, then a campfire that heals 30% (24 HP), then the boss.
  • Three card offers after each win. The AI tries each card (and skipping) in 8 short trial fights against the next floor's monsters and 8 against the boss, then takes the best.
  • One loss ends the run. Every player plays the same seeded runs, so two players can be compared run by run.
  • Not in it: events, shops, gold, the map, relics beyond Burning Blood, potions. Half the runs offer cards the AI's net never trained on.

Easy: Cultist, Jaw Worm, two Louses, small slimes. Hard: Looter, Large Slime, Lots of Slimes, three Louses, two Fungi Beasts, Exordium Thugs, Exordium Wildlife. Elites: Gremlin Nob, Lagavulin, three Sentries. Bosses: Slime Boss, The Guardian, Hexaghost.

Cards offered (31): Anger, Bloodletting, Body Slam, Brutality, Carnage, Clash, Cleave, Clothesline, Combust, Demon Form, Dropkick, Flex, FTL, Go for the Eyes, Heavy Blade, Hemokinesis, Inflame, Iron Wave, Metallicize, Perfected Strike, Pommel Strike, Pummel, Rage, Rupture, Shrug It Off, Spot Weakness, Sword Boomerang, Thunderclap, Twin Strike, Uppercut, Wild Strike.

Six findings

FINDING 1

HP at the boss is worth most where that boss's cliff is

We replayed 474 real boss fights from the headline runs, changing only the HP the player walked in with. The result is cause, not correlation: it explains 87% of the slope you see in normal runs.

Slime Boss is almost safe from 50 HP (0.92). The Guardian is a cliff: 0.07 at 30 HP, 0.49 at 50, 0.88 at 70. Hexaghost climbs slowly and is still only 0.90 at 80.

FINDING 2

An elite costs more than it pays, when it pays no relic

The two elites take 17.3 and 13.8 HP per won fight. Hard hallway fights take 0.5 to 3.6, easy ones about nothing. That HP is what the boss is missing.

Swap every elite for a hard hallway fight and the run ends with 14.0 more HP, and clears go from 0.720 to 0.920. Even with an extra card offer after each elite and a mid-act heal, skipping still clears more (+0.054, about 4 standard errors).

In this test an elite pays one card offer and no relic. That makes skipping the ceiling of a path choice here, not advice for real runs. The real-game controller paths "monsters first, elites last" for the same reason.

FINDING 3

At the last campfire, just rest

The test's one campfire sits right before the boss. A careful chooser played 64 boss fights for resting and for each possible upgrade, and upgraded only when it was clearly better. It upgraded in 2 runs of 50 and gained nothing: −0.10 HP, the same 0.720 clears.

The heal is 24 HP at 80 max HP, right before the boss, and the boss curve above says those 24 HP are worth a lot. A campfire earlier in the act, with more fights left to use an upgrade, could come out differently.

FINDING 5

Thinking deeper beat training harder

Same net, same 500 runs: one turn deep it clears 0.680, two turns deep 0.834 (+0.154, se 0.023). Most of the saved runs are at the second elite and the boss.

Training did not keep up. Self-play stopped improving after one round. 80,000 training games did no better than 40,000. A round trained on the two-turn player's own games played 1.0 to 1.4 HP worse. Every gain on the Act 1 test since 29 October came from how the AI searches and picks cards: the HP price, two turns, trial-fight card picks, looking at the boss when picking.

FINDING 6

New numbers are free. New mechanics cost.

The project's first goal is an AI that copes with content it has never seen, like a mod. To measure that, official elites were given changes and the net was compared with the hand-written player on them. Bigger numbers cost the net almost nothing extra. A mechanic it never trained against does: Sharp Hide 2 (you take damage for every Attack you play) costs it 6.3 HP per elite fight more than it costs the hand-written player.

Demon Form, the villain of the project. Of ten real cards the nets never trained on, only Demon Form cost them: −4.26 HP against the hand-written player on decks that held it. Its Strength arrives at the start of next turn, just past where a one-turn search looks. Scoring the position after turn-start effects fixed it with no training at all, and is now part of the product.

How it plays

It never picks a card. It picks where to end up.

The engine lists everything you could do, works out exactly where each choice leads, and a scoring function picks the best place to be. Hover a step to see where it sits in the search.

  1. Options

    Every legal play: each card on each target, each potion, End turn. Copies merge, so three Strikes against two monsters are two options, not six.

  2. Where each one leads

    The engine plays each option on a copy of the fight. A power the AI has never heard of still shows its effect in the result: more block, more damage, less monster HP.

  3. Chance is out in the open

    Draws and monster move rolls are chance points. The draw pile is a bag with no hidden order, so the AI can't peek. It lists every outcome with its odds, or samples a few.

  4. One turn deep

    After End turn the monsters' shown attacks land, and the search stops just before the next draw. That paused position gets a score.

  5. Two turns deep

    The product doesn't stop there. It samples 4 possible next hands and monster rolls and searches the next turn too, up to 400,000 positions per decision.

  6. The score

    A small net (about 190,000 weights) guesses how the fight will end: a loss is 0, a win is 2 minus HP lost / 100, and each HP kept earns a little extra. It reads every card as what it does, not its name, so a new card built from familiar parts already means something.

  7. Commit to the line

    The product plays the whole line its first search found, up to the next draw or random event, and only then searches again. Same decisions in 526 of 526 test decisions, at 70% of the search time.

  8. Picking cards

    No draft table. For each offer it plays 8 trial fights against the next floor and 8 against the boss with each option in the deck, and keeps the deck that scored best.

The net learned from games it played itself: first from searches scored by a hand-written formula, then from searches with its own previous version. It is the net trained on 29 October, and it is still the product's net today.

Read the screen

CommunicationMod hands a program the game's state. The controller turns it into an engine fight: HP, energy, powers, relics, every pile, each monster's intent and move history. The draw pile is sorted first, because a player can't see its order.

Think and play

The product's search, two turns deep, with the net trained from scratch on games that include the real Act 1 cards, since real rewards offer them. Each move goes back as a game command, and the controller checks the game's next state against what the engine predicted. It compares only when every one of the engine's samples agrees, so the match rates cover chance-free steps. A step with chance in it (a draw, a random target, a rolled monster move, so most monster turns) isn't compared: in run 83 the game showed a Mad Gremlin at 21 HP with 7 block where the engine had 17 HP and 11 block, and that fight still counted 202 of 202. Checking those steps against what the game showed is backlog 86.

What the live runs caught

Real games find what offline tests can't.
  • The Guardian's shift at the end of a turn. When Combust's end-of-turn damage breaks Mode Shift, the game queues the Guardian's 20 block behind the monsters' turn start, so the block lasts into your next turn. The engine added it at once and lost it with the turn. Found in run 8's boss fight, fixed in the engine the same morning.
  • Perfected Strike played by Havoc. Played from your hand, Perfected Strike counts itself as a Strike. Played by Havoc it is in limbo and doesn't. The engine always counted it and predicted 21 damage where the game dealt 18. Fixed in the engine: it counts the card only when it was played from the hand.
  • Regeneration heals first. The game puts Regeneration's heal at the top of its queue, so at the end of a turn it heals before Combust takes its HP. The engine did it the other way round, and near max HP the two disagreed by one point (game 84, engine 85). Fixed in the engine today: Regeneration and Orichalcum now act first at the end of a turn.
  • Blood for Blood after Sharp Hide. The Guardian's Sharp Hide hit for 3 as Blood for Blood+ was played. In the game the card is in limbo while that lands, so its own cost doesn't drop; the engine thought it was 1 cheaper, the play was refused, and the fallback rule lost run 16's boss fight from 75 HP. The controller now reads the cost the game shows, and the engine's rule for a card in limbo is queued for a fix.
  • Liquid Memories after Rampage. The AI played Rampage, then drank Liquid Memories within the same second to take it back at cost 0. The card was still flying to the discard pile, and landing reset its cost, so the game refused the play. The controller now trusts a "free" mark only when the game shows the card at cost 0, and remembers which free copies it played.
  • Skill Potion's free card. A Skill Potion gave Seeing Red at cost 0 for the turn. The card isn't in the deck list, so the controller had no base cost to compare. Cards made in a fight that the game shows at cost 0 are now marked free.
  • Snecko Oil. The potion rolls every card's cost for the fight. The controller copied the engine's own roll instead of the game's, so the plan asked for a 0-cost Strike the game didn't have. Cards now carry the cost the game shows.
  • Rampage forgot its damage in a shuffle. Rampage gains 5 damage each time it is played. The mark followed the pile, not the card, so a reshuffle dropped it and the engine predicted 8 damage where the game dealt 13. The mark now follows the game's own card id.
  • Havoc into Armaments. Havoc played the top card, Armaments, and the game opened its pick, which the engine's plan (built on a different random top card) had no answer for. The controller now takes the sampled outcome that matches the game's cards.
  • The rules version moved. The day's engine rule fixes changed the rules version to f59b662a, with five new recorded test games that pin each fix down.
  • Runic Dome. It hides every enemy intent, and without intents the controller can't rebuild a fight. The map rules now skip it as a boss relic.

What's next

What the live runs added: a decision and nine backlog entries

Each backlog entry says what it would change, how likely it is to work, what it costs, and the cheapest probe that would kill it fast.

Decided · Ascension 20 becomes the training default

The owner chose A20 for training from here on. At A0 the real game is too easy for the fighter (22 of the 24 Act 1 bosses it reached, in 27 runs); at A20 it wins about half, so that is where play has room to grow. Training, labels and fine-tunes will play A20; A0 and A20 data are never pooled; the main test moves to A20 once A20 baselines exist. First the training scripts need an ascension setting and the datasets need to record it.

82 · Never pass an immediate win

Once a fight has cost a lot of HP, the search can score carrying on above killing the last monster: run 56 left a Blue Slaver at 6 HP from turn 7 to turn 18. Cap an unfinished position's score at the best finish its HP allows. The bridge's fight guard covers it live meanwhile. Rated likely.

83 · Price each fight for this deck

The map prices every hallway fight at 15 HP and every elite at 40, for any deck, so a weak deck walks into Lagavulin. Use the fight net, plus a few trial fights for close calls, to price each fight for the deck and HP at hand: a separate run layer that uses the fighter as a tool. Rated even; under half a CPU-hour to test.

84 · Search the promising moves first

The owner's idea. When a two-turn search hits its node cap, the moves cut are whichever come last in hand order. Expand them in the order the one-turn search ranks them, and prune chance outcomes that can't change the choice. Rated even for better play, likely for speed.

85 · Break stalls

Run 83 fought four gremlins for 84 turns: within two turns, killing the Shield Gremlin that kept re-blocking its ally never looked worth it. Make progress visible to the score, or as a stopgap hand a fight past about 30 turns to the fallback. Rated likely.

86 · Check the chance steps too

Today's match rates skip any step with chance in it. Once the game shows a draw or a roll, replay the engine with that outcome forced and compare the rest, as its own count. Rated likely to surface monster-turn differences the count hides now.

87 · Work out card rewards early

Low priority, the owner's idea. The game's card reward is fixed by its seed before the fight starts, so the bridge could compute the pick while the game animates. A cache only: the fight never reads it. About 5% of a run's time to save.

79 · Answer every pick from the game

When a random card opens a pick (Havoc into Armaments, Warcry, Distilled Chaos), the bridge answers from the closest of the engine's 32 predicted samples, because the engine can't rebuild a fight paused at a pick. Teach it to, and every pick comes from the game's own state. Rated likely; engine work, no CPU.

80 · Ascension 20 for the net

The net has never trained at A20, and its first A20 Slime Boss losses arrived with 49 to 64 HP and lost up to 33 HP in a single monster turn. First size the gap with the test run at A20; then A20 in training or in the card picker's trial fights; and a run layer that weighs HP, plus gold, which the bot never spends. Rated even.

81 · Show the net the whole schedule

The owner's idea. The net sees each monster's intent on show and nothing after it, so a threat past the search horizon, like Hexaghost's turn-9 Inferno, is invisible. Give it the schedule ahead: turns until the biggest hit, its damage, Strength gained before it, the place in the cycle. Rated even; about 3 CPU-hours to test.
