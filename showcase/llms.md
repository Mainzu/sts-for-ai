### The engine and the first question

**How the work was done.** The owner and Claude worked side by side in a spec-first loop: the owner confirms a spec, Claude builds it step by step against checks a program can run, and every design call goes into a decisions log. That log kept growing for the whole project and holds 499 entries today.

##### Built

- A combat engine in Rust: Ironclad, Act 1, Ascension 0. 24 cards, the hallway monsters, Gremlin Nob, Lagavulin, three Sentries, Slime Boss with its split.

- Two twin nets trained on 30,000 games: one reads cards by what they do, one by their names.

- The Silent as a second character, to test transfer.

##### Learned

- On decks with five held-out cards (Twin Strike, Iron Wave, Thunderclap, Pommel Strike, Uppercut), the "what it does" net predicted fight outcomes with 50% less error. [setting: prediction error · 10-23 | Check 6: value-prediction error on games with held-out cards, effects net vs ids net, both trained on the same 30,000 games; bar 20%. Commit 39de9ebf, 2026-10-23.]

- Nets trained only on Ironclad predicted Silent fights with 27% less error than the names net. [setting: prediction error · Silent · 10-24 | Check 8: Silent tier-A decks, MSE 0.0753 vs 0.1034, bar 20%. Commit 647225dd, 2026-10-24.]

- Valuing an unseen power card missed its bar (18.3% against 20%). Demon Form was the hardest.

**Ruler** How far a net's predicted fight score is from the real one, on cards it never trained on.

**Moment:** Hemokinesis deals 15, not 16, with Rupture in play. The game works out the card's damage before Rupture's Strength lands, and the engine copies that.

Behind the scenes

## How the work was done

A one-person hobby project, set up so the research could keep going while its owner was away. The way of working changed almost as often as the tests did. Here is how it grew, step by step.

1. 23 Oct

**Spec first.** The owner writes what they want, Claude writes a spec, the owner confirms it, and Claude builds it in steps that each end in a check a program can run. Every design call goes into a decisions log.

2. 25 Oct

**Overnight sweeps.** Training recipes run in batches at night, some on Google Colab; the morning is for reading results.

3. 29 Oct

**A manager for the time away.** One Claude session keeps a manager file with its state and decides on the owner's behalf, with standing instructions from the owner: decide, record why and the alternatives, prefer what can be undone, and keep work moving without waiting on the owner.

4. 30 Oct

**A job queue and big-picture reviews.** Experiments go into a queue that runs them unattended. After the owner asked for faster progress and more data analysis, a saved review workflow was set up to run about once a day and after any major result: several analyst agents read all the data through different lenses, a synthesis follows, and skeptic agents check the key claims. 13 such reviews ran.

5. 2 Nov

**A fact store, a product registry, a training rule.** Facts go into a store of sealed claims that cite their source files and turn "suspect" when a source changes; the best player lives in one registry file; training runs only when the net has something new to learn.

6. 3 Nov

**Roles and lanes.** The manager is split into scheduled roles (below). Research runs in lanes: short projects with a question, a predicted chance of success, a CPU-hour cap, an end date and a written "reopen if". A dashboard shows the machine, the queue and every session.

7. 5 Nov

**One turn deep for comparisons.** The owner's choice: every change is judged one turn deep, where a read costs minutes; the two-turn product is the reported score, not the ruler.

9. 7 Nov

**Reads without a session.** A family of queued jobs can run from one session to its verdict, so a lane needs no worker checking in between jobs.

10. 9 Nov

**The owner works on Act 1 content directly.** A lane spent about 3.5 hours of spec, review and speed checks on two cards, so the owner moved Act 1 content into their own sessions.

11. 13 Nov

**Curator, "level is a pass".** A daily curator role keeps the fact store current. A change that is more correct or more general passes when it reads level, without a gain.

#### What worked

- **Speed of trying.** 55 lanes closed in 11 days, most within a day, each with a question, a cap, a prediction and a written verdict.

- **Honest records.** Failures were written up as carefully as wins, with what would justify reopening them. This page's "everything tried" list exists because of that.

- **It caught its own mistakes.** The night of 28 October's passes were shown to be seed luck the next day; a boss cost on the mutated-monster test turned out to rest on one lucky control and was withdrawn by a read five times larger.

- **Goal 1 moved.** The owner weighted unseen content at 0.8 of development. On it the lanes went from "new mechanics cost the net" to nets that remove about three quarters of the cost of mechanics they never saw.

- **Engine work landed fast:** content as data files, a smaller game state, all 25 Act 1 monsters and all 75 Ironclad cards by 14 November.

#### What didn't

- **The headline didn't move.** The product stayed the same from 3 to 13 November; about 865 of 1,550 booked thread-hours ran after 3 November without a change to it. Every gain on the Act 1 test came from search and card picks, and most gains found one turn deep shrank to about a third of their size two turns deep.

- **The machine sat idle.** About 70% of its thread-hours went unused, while the rules called machine time the bottleneck.

- **Tests reversed each other.** The recipe that ranked first on one test ranked fourth on the gauntlet; each ruler left out a part of what matters, so rulers kept changing.

- **Coordination cost.** A lane needed hours of process for two cards, so the owner did that work in their own sessions.

- **Facts go stale.** One rules change on 13 November flagged 31 stored facts for re-checking.

How it plays

## It never picks a card. It picks where to end up.

The engine lists everything you could do, works out exactly where each choice leads, and a scoring function picks the best place to be.

1.

#### Options

Every legal play: each card on each target, each potion, End turn. Copies merge, so three Strikes against two monsters are two options, not six.

2.

#### Where each one leads

The engine plays each option on a copy of the fight. A power the AI has never heard of still shows its effect in the result: more block, more damage, less monster HP.

3.

#### Chance is out in the open

Draws and monster move rolls are chance points. The draw pile is a bag with no hidden order, so the AI can't peek. It lists every outcome with its odds, or samples a few.

4.

#### One turn deep

After End turn the monsters' shown attacks land, and the search stops just before the next draw. That paused position gets a score.

5.

#### Two turns deep

The product doesn't stop there. It samples 4 possible next hands and monster rolls and searches the next turn too, up to 400,000 positions per decision.

6.

#### The score

A small net (about 190,000 weights) guesses how the fight will end: a loss is 0, a win is 2 minus HP lost / 100, and each HP kept earns a little extra. It reads every card as what it does, not its name, so a new card built from familiar parts already means something.

7.

#### Commit to the line

The product plays the whole line its first search found, up to the next draw or random event, and only then searches again. Same decisions in 526 of 526 test decisions, at 70% of the search time.

8.

#### Picking cards

No draft table. For each offer it plays 8 trial fights against the next floor and 8 against the boss with each option in the deck, and keeps the deck that scored best.

The net learned from games it played itself: first from searches scored by a hand-written formula, then from searches with its own previous version. It is the net trained on 29 October, and it is still the product's net today.

#### Read the screen

CommunicationMod hands a program the game's state. The controller turns it into an engine fight: HP, energy, powers, relics, every pile, each monster's intent and move history. The draw pile is sorted first, because a player can't see its order.

#### Think and play

The product's search, two turns deep, with the net trained from scratch on games that include the real Act 1 cards, since real rewards offer them. Each move goes back as a game command, and the controller checks the game's next state against what the engine predicted. It compares only when every one of the engine's samples agrees, so the match rates cover chance-free steps. A step with chance in it (a draw, a random target, a rolled monster move, so most monster turns) isn't compared: in run 83 the game showed a Mad Gremlin at 21 HP with 7 block where the engine had 17 HP and 11 block, and that fight still counted 202 of 202. Checking those steps against what the game showed is backlog 86.

#### Everything outside fights

Since 06:02 the controller plays whole runs: Neow, the map (monsters first, elites last), rewards, card picks by trial fights, campfires (rest under 60% HP, 75% before the boss), and walks past shops. A fight with an unknown monster falls back to a simple rule. From run 22 on, a run stops when Act 2 starts, on purpose: the engine doesn't model Act 2, so the loop relaunches the game for the next run. Those endings are not deaths. The run layer's rules changed during the day (versions 2 to 5, from run 35), and from run 58 the fight guard takes a winning move the search passed; each setting keeps its own table below.

### What the live runs caught
Real games find what offline tests can't.

- **The Guardian's shift at the end of a turn.** When Combust's end-of-turn damage breaks Mode Shift, the game queues the Guardian's 20 block behind the monsters' turn start, so the block lasts into your next turn. The engine added it at once and lost it with the turn. Found in run 8's boss fight, fixed in the engine the same morning.

- **Perfected Strike played by Havoc.** Played from your hand, Perfected Strike counts itself as a Strike. Played by Havoc it is in limbo and doesn't. The engine always counted it and predicted 21 damage where the game dealt 18. Fixed in the engine: it counts the card only when it was played from the hand.

- **Regeneration heals first.** The game puts Regeneration's heal at the top of its queue, so at the end of a turn it heals before Combust takes its HP. The engine did it the other way round, and near max HP the two disagreed by one point (game 84, engine 85). Fixed in the engine today: Regeneration and Orichalcum now act first at the end of a turn.

- **Blood for Blood after Sharp Hide.** The Guardian's Sharp Hide hit for 3 as Blood for Blood+ was played. The controller now reads the cost the game shows, and the engine's rule for a card in limbo is queued for a fix.

- **Liquid Memories after Rampage.** The AI played Rampage, then drank Liquid Memories within the same second to take it back at cost 0. The card was still flying to the discard pile, and landing reset its cost, so the game refused the play. The controller now trusts a "free" mark only when the game shows the card at cost 0, and remembers which free copies it played.

- **Snecko Oil.** The potion rolls every card's cost for the fight. The controller copied the engine's own roll instead of the game's, so the plan asked for a 0-cost Strike the game didn't have. Cards now carry the cost the game shows.

- **Rampage forgot its damage in a shuffle.** Rampage gains 5 damage each time it is played. The mark followed the pile, not the card, so a reshuffle dropped it and the engine predicted 8 damage where the game dealt 13. The mark now follows the game's own card id.

What's next

## What the live runs added: a decision and nine backlog entries

Each backlog entry says what it would change, how likely it is to work, what it costs, and the cheapest probe that would kill it fast.

#### Decided · Ascension 20 becomes the training default

The owner chose A20 for training from here on. At A0 the real game is too easy for the fighter (22 of the 24 Act 1 bosses it reached, in 27 runs); at A20 it wins about half, so that is where play has room to grow. Training, labels and fine-tunes will play A20; A0 and A20 data are never pooled; the main test moves to A20 once A20 baselines exist. First the training scripts need an ascension setting and the datasets need to record it.

#### 82 · Never pass an immediate win

Once a fight has cost a lot of HP, the search can score carrying on above killing the last monster: run 56 left a Blue Slaver at 6 HP from turn 7 to turn 18. Cap an unfinished position's score at the best finish its HP allows. The bridge's fight guard covers it live meanwhile. Rated likely.

#### 83 · Price each fight for this deck

The map prices every hallway fight at 15 HP and every elite at 40, for any deck, so a weak deck walks into Lagavulin. Use the fight net, plus a few trial fights for close calls, to price each fight for the deck and HP at hand: a separate run layer that uses the fighter as a tool. Rated even; under half a CPU-hour to test.

#### 84 · Search the promising moves first

The owner's idea. When a two-turn search hits its node cap, the moves cut are whichever come last in hand order. Expand them in the order the one-turn search ranks them, and prune chance outcomes that can't change the choice. Rated even for better play, likely for speed.

#### 85 · Break stalls

Run 83 fought four gremlins for 84 turns: within two turns, killing the Shield Gremlin that kept re-blocking its ally never looked worth it. Make progress visible to the score, or as a stopgap hand a fight past about 30 turns to the fallback. Rated likely.

#### 86 · Check the chance steps too

Today's match rates skip any step with chance in it. Once the game shows a draw or a roll, replay the engine with that outcome forced and compare the rest, as its own count. Rated likely to surface monster-turn differences the count hides now.

#### 87 · Work out card rewards early

Low priority, the owner's idea. The game's card reward is fixed by its seed before the fight starts, so the bridge could compute the pick while the game animates. A cache only: the fight never reads it. About 5% of a run's time to save.

#### 79 · Answer every pick from the game

When a random card opens a pick (Havoc into Armaments, Warcry, Distilled Chaos), the bridge answers from the closest of the engine's 32 predicted samples, because the engine can't rebuild a fight paused at a pick. Teach it to, and every pick comes from the game's own state. Rated likely; engine work, no CPU.

#### 80 · Ascension 20 for the net

The net has never trained at A20, and its first A20 Slime Boss losses arrived with 49 to 64 HP and lost up to 33 HP in a single monster turn. First size the gap with the test run at A20; then A20 in training or in the card picker's trial fights; and a run layer that weighs HP, plus gold, which the bot never spends. Rated even.

#### 81 · Show the net the whole schedule

The owner's idea. The net sees each monster's intent on show and nothing after it, so a threat past the search horizon, like Hexaghost's turn-9 Inferno, is invisible. Give it the schedule ahead: turns until the biggest hit, its damage, Strength gained before it, the place in the cycle. Rated even; about 3 CPU-hours to test.

### Period 6 (P6): Real Act 1 goes in (6 to 9 November)

- **Fine-tunes on as few as 11 fights** [project history · 6 to 9 November] In period 6 the lab mostly stopped training nets from scratch. Instead it fine-tuned one existing net in short bursts on new content's fights: 127 fine-tunes against 8 from-scratch runs. Of the 127 bursts, 94 used 99 fights or fewer, and 12 used just 11. The question had shifted from 'what is the best net?' to 'how few fights does the net need to adapt to new content?'.
- **Retraining the same recipe with a new seed moves the score as much as most ideas did** [project history · 6 to 9 November] On 6-7 Nov the lanes read 9 training recipes, each trained with 3 to 5 different seeds, on the same 500 gauntlet sequences. For three recipes they also read the same nets a second way (@tsl1). Seeds of the same recipe differed by 1.4 to 5.2 points of clear rate (median 2.0). The 10-29 net's whole lead over the heuristic on these sequences is 3.8 points (0.634 vs 0.596). So the luck of a single training seed could make an idea look like it gained or lost most of that lead. This is why the project read seed twins before believing a result.
- **22 new Ironclad cards the net had never seen** [project history · 6 to 9 November] When the real Act 1 Ironclad cards went in on 7 November, the 29 October net was tested on 22 cards it had never trained on, with three copies of each card put into the deck. On 19 of them it lost nothing against the hand-written player: 18 read level, and with Berserk it pulled ahead (+0.066 wins). Berserk made both players worse, but the hand-written player got worse by more, and it played Berserk on only 15% of the turns it held one. Three cards cost the net: Impervious (-0.122 wins), Disarm (-0.118) and Intimidate (-0.062). All three are Exhaust skills whose whole value is a number the net must read: 30 Block, negative enemy Strength, Weak on every enemy.
- **Made-up monster powers: the trigger orders the cost** [project history · 6 to 9 November] The 24 made-up elite powers combine 4 triggers with 7 effects (Strength, Block, Damage, PlainDamage, Weak, Vulnerable, Frail), minus the 4 pairs that real monsters already have. On average, the time a power fires orders its cost more than what it does. Powers that fire at the monster's own turn start or end cost the net 0.39 and 0.53 HP per elite fight, measured beyond a stat-only fight of matching difficulty. Powers that punish the player's own actions cost 1.62 (playing a card) and 2.98 (hitting the monster). The effect still matters: Weak costs over 2 HP on every trigger. Three mechanics are left out because the hand-written player collapsed on them. Against 'Vulnerable to the player on every card played' it won only 69% of elite fights, while the net won 85%.

### Period 7 (P7): New nets for new content; relics and potions (10 to 12 November)

- **Luck is a fifth of the noise, and subtracting it didn't help** [project history · 10 to 12 November] On 11-11 the lab measured how much of a training label is luck. Replaying 1,200 positions 8 times each showed that luck after the decision is 19% of the labels' spread, not most of it. Subtracting the net's estimate of that luck removed only 13% of it (1% on Demon Form decks). On paired comparisons of two players over 100 card sequences, the luck-corrected difference was more spread out than the raw one (standard deviation 12.0 HP against 8.5). It needed a ratio of 0.75 or lower and got 1.41.
- **Four made-up powers on one elite at once** [project history · 10 to 12 November] Around midnight of 10-11 November the lab stacked four made-up monster powers on a single official elite, on 40 different random combinations. The 29 October net and a net trained from scratch on the real Act 1 pack both lost about 6 HP per fight more than the hand-written player, beyond what the plain elite costs. The net trained on a pack of made-up mechanics lost about half as much, 3.16 HP, on combinations it had never met together.

### Period 8 (P8): The product changes; the real game (13 to 14 November)

#### Behind the scenes

- **636 stored facts, 48 now suspect** [project history · 13 to 14 November] By 11-14 the project's fact store (docs/claims) held 636 facts: 426 active, 210 retired. Measurements are the bulk (246 active). Lane facts are almost all retired (161 of 169) because a lane's facts retire when it closes. The real-game day left 48 active facts suspect: the store flags a fact automatically when a file it cites changes. 13 of the 34 'how' facts (how the code works) went suspect, because the day's rule fixes touched engine files (state.rs, monsters.rs, registry.rs) and scripts/gauntlet.py. Every file those facts cite was last changed on 11-14. The most-rewritten fact is lane/act1-content/step, revised 59 times as the Act 1 content work moved block by block.

#### What it thinks hardest about

- **Mostly instant, now and then a long think** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] At Ascension 20 the AI makes a typical move in 0.15 seconds, and three moves in four take under a second. The slowest 10% of moves, though, used 73% of all its thinking time, and one move in a hundred took more than 28 seconds.
- **Slime Boss is the fight it thinks hardest about** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] In a typical Slime Boss fight the AI thought for 81 seconds in total. In a typical Hexaghost fight it thought for 2 seconds, and against Gremlin Nob for about 1. Four of the five A20 fights with the most total thinking time were Slime Boss fights, and one of them took 7 minutes 20 seconds.
- **Every extra monster slows it down** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] With one monster left, the AI averages 1.22 seconds a move and only 4% of moves hit the search's node cap. With four on the board (gremlin gangs, slime splits) it averages 7.57 seconds, and 39% of those moves hit the cap.
- **When the search runs out of room** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] The search stops when it reaches a fixed number of positions (its node cap). At A20 that happened on 8.7% of moves (13.9% at A0). It hit the cap on 43% of the moves where it drank a potion, against 5% of the moves that played a Strike.
- **The single hardest moves** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Three of the AI's five slowest A20 moves came against Slime Boss on turn 3, though two of them were back-to-back moves in the same turn of one fight. In that turn it held Ascender's Bane, Bash+, a Strike and two Defends and thought for 96 seconds before playing Strike. Its slowest move overall was at A0, against three Red Louses, two still with Curl Up: it thought for 111 seconds and then played a single Strike.

#### What it picks in the real game

- **A20: the cards it grabs when offered** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] At Ascension 20 the AI takes Juggernaut (6 of 8 offers), Impervious (6 of 9) and Rampage (6 of 10) more often than not. Its most-taken card overall is the common Armaments, picked 25 times out of 46 offers, more than any other card.
- **A20: the cards it won't touch** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] Warcry was the card it was offered most at Ascension 20 (60 times) and it took it only 6 times. Limit Break, Entrench, Sentinel, Dark Embrace and Dual Wield were offered 14-17 times each and never taken. Barricade and Bloodletting got picked once each in 14 offers.
- **A0: loved and never picked** [real game · A0] At Ascension 0 Fire Breathing (5 of 7) and Disarm (7 of 10) were its top picks. Body Slam was offered 23 times and never taken, and Dark Embrace (11 offers) and Sever Soul (10) were also never taken.
- **How often it skips the whole card reward** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] About one card reward in five, the AI takes nothing: it rates 'skip' above every card offered. At Ascension 0 it got pickier late in the act (about a third skipped on floors 13-17); at Ascension 20 the skip rate barely moves with the floor.
- **It doesn't chase rares** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Rare cards don't excite it: at Ascension 20 it took 29% of the rares it could pick, against 27% of commons and 27% of uncommons. At Ascension 0, uncommons were its favourite rarity (31%), and rares were no more popular than commons (26% against 25%).
- **A20: the deck at the Act 1 boss, boss by boss** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] Which boss a run met matters more than the cards. Hexaghost was beaten 4 times in 17 fights; Slime Boss and The Guardian together were beaten 26 times in 34. Most of the raw gap between cards comes from which boss each deck met. Wild Strike decks won only 3 of 9 boss fights, but 5 of those 9 were Hexaghost fights, all lost. Against Slime Boss or The Guardian, Wild Strike decks won 3 of 4, close to the 23 of 30 for the other decks. Fire Breathing stands out against both kinds of boss. Fire Breathing decks beat Hexaghost 2 times in 3, against 2 in 14 for the other decks. They also won all 5 of their fights against Slime Boss or The Guardian. Clash (10 of 11), Spot Weakness (8 of 9) and Pommel Strike (11 of 13) decks also beat Slime Boss and The Guardian more often than decks without those cards. Spot Weakness decks met Hexaghost only once, and Pommel Strike decks twice, so their good overall records partly reflect easier bosses.

#### What hurts

- **What ended the A20 runs** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] Of the 63 Ascension 20 runs, 33 died in Act 1. Hexaghost alone ended 13 of them: 13 of the 17 runs that reached it. Lagavulin was the deadliest elite: it ended 6 of the 17 runs that fought it (18 fights), against 2 deaths in 21 Gremlin Nob fights.
- **HP lost per encounter at A20** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] In the A20 hallways, the Gremlin Gang cost about 18 HP a fight on average, twice a lone Jaw Worm's 8.8, though a few bad fights pull that average up (median 12). A Cultist cost only 3.4 HP: those fights end fast, before Ritual's Strength has stacked much.
- **Same monster, two Ascensions** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Every elite cost more HP at Ascension 20 than at A0. Gremlin Nob went from 11 to 32 HP, but its A0 figure rests on only 3 fights. Lagavulin went from 24.9 to 32.1 HP and won 9 of 9 at A0, but lost 6 of its 18 fights at A20.

#### How it plays

- **Strike and Defend are six in ten of all cards played** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] At Ascension 20 the bot played 7,792 cards, and 36% of them were plain Strikes and 25% plain Defends. The first card it picked up from a reward to make the top five is Anger. At Ascension 0 the picture is almost the same: Strike 35%, Defend 23%, then Anger at 5%.
- **Whirlwind always gets played; Evolve rarely does** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] When a card sits in the opening hand of a turn, how often does the bot play it that turn? Whirlwind gets played 92% of the time and Flex 88%. Evolve is played only 21% of the time, Fire Breathing about a third of the time, and even Bash only 44%. Strike (74%) gets played far more often than Defend (57%). At A0 the same pattern holds: Evolve 14%, Demon Form and Fire Breathing 26%.
- **Bash on turn 1? It depends who is across the table** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] With Bash in the opening hand at A20, the bot played it on turn 1 in 113 of 207 fights and played it as its first card 70 times. It Bashed every Cultist (18 of 18), every Gremlin Nob (7 of 7) and every Slime Boss (6 of 6). It mostly held Bash against lice (2 of 11 against a Green and Red Louse pair, 0 of 6 against a lone Red Louse), against Lagavulin (1 of 7) and against an Acid Slime (M) with a Spike Slime (S) (2 of 10). Lice curl up and Lagavulin starts asleep, which may be part of why, but the data doesn't show the reason.
- **It rarely Defends against Gremlin Nob** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] Gremlin Nob's Enrage gives it Strength every time you play a Skill. The bot plays its Defends sparingly there: in 74 Nob turns with a Defend in hand it played one on only 17 (23%), against 154 of 216 (71%) at the other elites (Lagavulin and Sentries). Across all Nob turns it played 23 Defends to 101 Strikes, the lowest Defend-to-Strike ratio of any enemy group. It won 19 of its 21 Nob fights. The other end of the scale is Acid Slime (L) and The Guardian, where it blocked more than it struck.
- **Potions go down early, often on turn 1** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] At A20 the bot drank 242 potions, and 176 of them (73%) on turn 1 of a fight. Its HP at the time was usually high: the median was 82% of max HP, and only 17 potions were drunk below half HP. It drank more potions per fight at bosses (25 in 51 fights) and elites (31 in 54) than in hallway fights (186 in 477). Energy, Weak and Fear Potions top the list. Fairy in a Bottle, which works on its own, sat in the belt for 120 turns.
- **Never dies with a potion** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Whenever the engine played a losing fight to the end, its potion belt was empty when the run died: 33 of 33 deaths at Ascension 20 and 2 of 2 at Ascension 0 in Act 1. The third A0 Act 1 death (run 16, a boss fight handed to the fallback rule partway through) also had an empty belt. The Act 2 fights at A0 were played by a fallback rule that never drinks potions, and 13 of those 16 deaths still had potions unused.
- **Ending the turn with energy and a playable card** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] One 84-turn gremlin standoff (run 83, floor 5) is set aside here because it alone gave a quarter of the cases. Without it, the bot at A20 ended 8.9% of its turns with energy left over, and 5.3% with both energy and a card it could afford to play. The leftover card was usually a Strike. Without that fight Havoc almost never shows up (1 case). The rate depends a lot on the enemy. With The Guardian or a lone Acid Slime (L) on the board it was about 17%. With Jaw Worm, Cultist or Hexaghost it was close to 0. A0 is almost the same once its one long stall (run 18, floor 2, a lone Acid Slime (S)) is set aside: 8.8% and 5.2%. With that fight counted, both levels sit at 10.3% energy left and 6.8-6.9% energy plus an affordable card.

#### Records

- **The longest fights: an 84-turn gremlin standoff** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] The longest real-game fight lasted 84 turns. In A20 run 83 (floor 5), a Shield Gremlin with 4 HP shielded the remaining Mad Gremlin, giving it 11 Block every turn, for about 69 turns (turns 14 to 82). On 67 of those turns the net struck the shielded Mad Gremlin rather than the 4-HP Shield Gremlin. The two strikes scored almost the same (1.51 vs 1.49). Its HP drained from 35 to 15 before it finally killed the Shield Gremlin, and it won on 8 HP. The A0 record has a different cause. In run 18, floor 2, a Small Acid Slime sat at 4 HP for about 23 turns while the net played Defend, Defend, End. Every option scored exactly the same (2.01), so to the search killing the slime looked no better than waiting.
- **Closest shaves: wins on 3 HP** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] The net won two A20 fights with 3 HP left: three Fungi Beasts on floor 13 of run 34, and Slime Boss in run 44 after starting the fight on 66 HP. Some wins started from almost nothing. Run 34 entered a Red Slaver fight on 9 HP and left on 4, and run 33 beat a Blue Slaver from 12 HP. At A0 only one fight the net finished ended on 10 HP or less: Hexaghost on 7 HP.
- **The hardest single enemy turn** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] The biggest single enemy turn took 38 HP: Slime Boss's Slam in A20 run 39, and the net still won that fight. Hexaghost fills much of the top of the list, with Divider (6 hits of 7 when the player is near full HP) and its 6x6 multi-hit. A hallway fight also made the top three: in run 52 on floor 5, a Gremlin Wizard's Ultimate Blast plus two Fat Gremlins took 37 HP in one turn.
- **Fastest boss kills, and Hexaghost's wall** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Slime Boss gives the net its fastest boss wins: in 4 turns twice at A0, and in 6 turns at A20, where its median win took 9. The Guardian's wins run long, a median of 13 turns at A20 and 12.5 at A0. Hexaghost's few A20 wins were quick, 8 to 10 turns.
- **Meat on the Bone's big nights** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] Burning Blood does steady work: +6 HP after nearly every win (507 of 549 A20 wins), 3,207 HP in all. The biggest single heals came from Burning Blood plus Meat on the Bone: +18 when a fight ends at half HP or lower. A20 run 77, the only A20 run that held Meat on the Bone, got that +18 in four of its five wins after picking it up, the last after The Guardian (23 to 41 HP). At A0, Black Blood (+12) shows up only in Act 2.
- **Flawless fights: lice yes, Jaw Worm no** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] At A20 the net finished about 1 in 7 hallway fights without losing HP: 68 of 471. Two Red Lice were the easiest at 4 of 12, and a Large Spike Slime was spotless 6 of 21 times. Jaw Worm almost always lands a hit (2 flawless of 48), and Medium Acid Slime + Small Spike Slime never went flawless in 23 fights. Elites and bosses at A20 had a single flawless win: Gremlin Nob in 3 turns (run 54, floor 14). At A0, Slime Boss fell without the net losing any HP 3 times out of 7.

#### What the net believes

- **When the net says 'won', it wins** [real game · A0 and A20, shown apart] At Ascension 20, the net scored 464 fights at 1.80 or higher at the start, and it won every one of them but one, a Lagavulin. Below 1.0 it expected to lose, and 21 of those 24 fights were lost. In the fights it won, it was a pessimist about HP: it expected to lose about 11 HP and actually lost about 7.6 (after Burning Blood's heal, which its score counts too).
- **Hexaghost and Lagavulin fool it; The Guardian scares it more than it should** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] At A20 the net's fight-start score was furthest above the real result against Hexaghost (13 of 17 fights lost) and Lagavulin (6 of 18 lost). Against The Guardian it was the other way round: in the 11 fights it won, its start score was worth about 70 HP lost, and it actually lost about 34 HP.
- **When looking one more turn ahead changed the verdict** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] In run 34 (A20), the Red Slaver fight on floor 14 started at 9 HP. One turn deep, the best option scored 0.15, which reads as an almost certain loss. Two turns deep, every attacking line scored about 1.52. The search played the Skill Potion, and the fight was won: 4 HP at the end, 10 after the end-of-fight heal. The reverse also happens: in run 78, against a Looter at 11 HP, one turn deep looked like a comfortable win at 1.58. Two turns deep it scored 0.43, and that fight was lost.
- **Looking two turns ahead: start with Strike, not Defend** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] About one decision in five, the two-turn search picked a first action that was not among the best-scoring ones one turn deep: 630 of 3,175 at A20 and 307 of 1,446 at A0. At A20 Strike was the two-turn pick in 351 of those 630 changes. Defend was a one-turn favourite overruled for Strike 138 times, and the reverse happened only 24 times.
- **Cards the net most wants to play rather than end the turn** [real game · A20 · rule versions pooled] At A20, when Rampage or Ghostly Armor was playable, the net typically scored playing it about 22 points above ending the turn right there. Whirlwind came last of the cards shown, at about 10, from only 4 runs.

Reading the numbers

## A short glossary

**Clears** The share of runs that beat the Act 1 boss. 0.834 means 417 of 500.

**End HP** HP left after the boss, 0 for a run that died. Used for paired comparisons.

**se** Standard error, the noise range of a number. A difference under about 2 se may be luck.

**One turn / two turns deep** How far the search looks: to the end of this turn, or through the next one with 4 sampled hands.

**Runs 0–499 / 500–999** Two fixed books of seeded runs. Settings were tuned on the first; the second checks them.

**The product** The best player the project makes: the 29 October net, two turns deep, with the HP price and line step.

**Hand-written player** A formula that scores positions from card and intent numbers. The baseline every net is compared with.

**Goal 1 and goal 2** Goal 1: play well on content the AI has never seen. Goal 2: be as strong at Slay the Spire as Stockfish is at chess.
