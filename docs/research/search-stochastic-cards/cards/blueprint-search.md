Note: every card below was read through WebFetch, which returns a small-model summary of the page. Numbers are copied from that summary; check them against the paper before any use. "Cited by" was not checked for these cards.

### On-line Policy Improvement using Monte-Carlo Search (1996; arXiv reprint 2025)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05407 (reprint of NeurIPS 1996 paper, pp. 1068-1074; venue taken from a search snippet, the reprint page itself does not name it) · **Venue:** NeurIPS 1996 (search snippet) · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Real-time policy improvement for an adaptive controller, shown on backgammon move choice: can a rollout-based search beat the base policy it was built from?
- **Method:** For each candidate action, play the game out many times with the base policy making all decisions, average the results, and take the best action. That is one rollout policy-improvement step. Trials are independent and parallel. Statistical pruning drops weak candidates early. Truncated rollouts stop after a few steps and use a neural net's equity estimate.
- **Assumes:** Stochastic dice; a perfect model of the randomness and the action-to-state mapping; full state information for the searcher; a simulator; the base policy is used for both sides in rollouts. Discrete turns, two players.
- **Claims:** Full rollouts (Table 1, points per game vs TD-Gammon 1-ply): Lin-1 base -0.52 to Monte-Carlo -0.01 (5 sec/move); Lin-2 -0.65 to -0.02 (5 sec/move); Lin-3 -0.32 to +0.04 (10 sec/move). Base players used 30K trials, Monte-Carlo players 5K trials. Equity loss per move (Table 2, ppg): Random 0.330 to 0.131 (ratio 2.5); Lin-1 0.040 to 0.0124 (ratio 3.2); Lin-2 0.0665 to 0.0175 (ratio 3.8); Lin-3 0.0291 to 0.00749 (ratio 3.9). Truncated rollouts (Table 3): 10 hidden units, base 0.0152 to 0.00318 thorough 11-step (ratio 4.8, 25 sec/move), or 0.00433 optimistic 11-step (ratio 3.5, 9 sec/move); 80 hidden units, base 0.0120 to 0.00181 thorough 7-step (ratio 6.6, 65 sec/move), or 0.00269 optimistic 7-step (ratio 4.5, 18 sec/move). CPU times on 32 SP1 nodes. Abstract: error rate of base players drops "by as much as a factor of 5 or more."
- **Evaluation:** Backgammon; base policies from random up to TD-Gammon; test set of about 800 positions for equity loss; roughly 25-30 base-player decisions per rollout trial; about 10K trials per candidate without pruning.
- **Code:** none found (arXiv license only)
- **Fit:** good. The cleanest measured example of one rollout-improvement step over a learned base policy under random outcomes, with numbers for gain versus compute. It breaks the constraints on the nearest reading: it uses a perfect model of the dice and the full state, so it does not show what hidden draw order does to rollouts.

### Combining Deep Reinforcement Learning and Search for Imperfect-Information Games (ReBeL) (2020)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13544 · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.GT); no peer-reviewed venue named on the page. A NeurIPS 2020 version exists (search snippet). · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** AlphaZero-style RL plus search does not carry over to imperfect-information games, since a state has no unique value once hidden information and action frequencies matter.
- **Method:** Search runs over public belief states (a distribution over hidden states given public history). Depth-limited subgames are solved with CFR-D; leaves use a learned value net that predicts values at a belief state. Self-play trains the net on the states it meets. Safe test-time search: sample a random iteration and pass down its beliefs.
- **Assumes:** Two-player zero-sum for the theory; rules and the agents' policies, including search, are common knowledge; random seeds are not; subgames have a fixed pre-set depth. Value-net input for poker: agent index, acting agent, pot relative to stacks, board, beliefs over both hands (2 x 1326). Liar's Dice: agent index, acting agent, last bid one-hot, beliefs over infostates.
- **Claims:** Heads-up no-limit hold'em, in thousandths of a big blind per game: Slumbot 45 +- 5; BabyTartanian8 9 +- 4; LBR 881 +- 94; Dong Kim (human, 7,500 hands, AIVAT) 165 +- 69 (raw -358 +- 188). Speed: "faster than 2 seconds per hand and never needed more than 5 seconds for a decision." Turn endgame: "equivalent to running about 125 iterations of full-game tabular CFR." Liar's Dice exploitability, depth-2 subgames, 1,024 search iterations, ReBeL CFR-D: 0.017 / 0.015 / 0.024 / 0.017 for the four game sizes, versus full-game CFR at 0.001 / 0.001 / 0.002 / 0.002 (1,024 iterations). No quantitative speed comparison with blueprint methods.
- **Evaluation:** Heads-up no-limit hold'em (matches vs Slumbot, BabyTartanian8, LBR, Dong Kim); turn endgame hold'em; Liar's Dice (1x4f, 1x5f, 1x6f, 2x3f).
- **Code:** github.com/facebookresearch/rebel, Liar's Dice only; poker code not released. License not stated.
- **Fit:** partial. Gives the search-over-a-learned-value-at-the-leaves pattern for hidden information, which fits a blueprint plus search. Its guarantees and the strongest results are two-player zero-sum poker. Net size and hardware are not stated in the page summary, and its value net takes a belief over hidden hands, which a hidden draw order would need to supply in some form.

### Depth-Limited Solving for Imperfect-Information Games (2018)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/1805.08195 · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (NeurIPS 2018 per common citation; not named on the page) · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Depth-limited search works in perfect-information games because leaves have values. In imperfect-information games leaves do not have a single value, so a blueprint's leaf values need a different treatment.
- **Method:** At the depth limit the opponent chooses among several strategies for the rest of the game (4 or 10 used), each giving a different leaf value. Strategies come from iterative best-response generation (first round) or from blueprint biases toward fold, call, or raise (second round). Leaves are valued by rollouts of stored strategies or by a DNN (34 inputs, 4 outputs, 2 hidden layers of 64). Solving is nested as play descends the tree, including off-tree actions.
- **Assumes:** Two-player zero-sum; a blueprint that approximates Nash, used for leaf values; player 1 has played the equilibrium strategy before the subgame. Perfect recall not mentioned in the page. Two betting rounds with abstraction.
- **Claims:** Head-to-head HUNL, mbb/g, 95% CI: blueprint only -57 +- 13 vs Baby Tartanian8, -11 +- 8 vs Slumbot; naive depth-limited -10 +- 8 and -1 +- 15; depth-limited solving (Modicum) +6 +- 5 and +11 +- 9. Compute: Modicum blueprint about 700 core hours and 16 GB; real-time play on a 4-core CPU at about 20 seconds per hand. Baby Tartanian8 about 2 million core hours and 18 TB; Slumbot about 250,000 core hours and 2 TB. Note: the page's text on DNN leaf values vs Baby Tartanian8 says the agent lost 11 +- 10 mbb/g; the summary flagged this sign as conflicting with the rollout result, so check the paper.
- **Evaluation:** Heads-up no-limit hold'em with abstraction; Baby Tartanian8 and Slumbot as opponents.
- **Code:** none found in the page
- **Fit:** partial. It is the clearest CPU-only blueprint-plus-search result (real-time 4-core CPU), and it shows that a blueprint's value at the depth limit can be replaced by a choice among stored strategies. It is two-player poker with a perfect model and a fixed abstraction; it does not cover a single-player game with random monsters.

### Improving Policies via Search in Cooperative Partially Observable Games (SPARTA) (2019/2020)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/1912.02318 · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.AI); AAAI 2020 per search snippet · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Improve a common blueprint policy in a cooperative partially observable game with limited communication (Hanabi) using search over beliefs.
- **Method:** One agent runs 1-ply Monte Carlo rollouts over its beliefs, assuming others follow the blueprint; multi-agent search uses a max-range budget on how many action-observation histories are searched. UCB-style pruning (after 100 rollouts per action, drop actions more than 2 std below the best) and a 0.05 deviation threshold from the blueprint cut rollouts by about 10x.
- **Assumes:** Fully cooperative; deterministic blueprint that is common knowledge; each agent knows the other agents' policies exactly (imperfect partner models listed as future work); exact belief is tractable because positive-probability states number up to about 10 million in Hanabi.
- **Claims:** Theorem 1: V^{pi_s} - V^{pi_b} >= -2 T Delta |A| N^{-1/2}, with N rollouts per step (a bound that search does not substantially reduce value). 2-player (Table 1, avg of 25 / % perfect): SAD 24.08 to 24.53 (single-agent) to 24.61 (multi-agent, MR = 2,000), 56.1% to 71.1% to 75.5%; SmartBot 22.99 to 24.21 to 24.46; DQN 23.45 to 24.30 to 24.49. Single-agent SAD by player count: 2p 24.08 to 24.53; 3p 23.99 to 24.61; 4p 23.81 to 24.49; 5p 23.01 to 23.91. Max-range trade-off (SmartBot): MR 0 gives 24.21 with about 1.5e5 rollouts; MR 10,000 gives 24.48 with about 1.8e8 rollouts. Compute: about 2 core-hours per game (single-agent), 90 core-hours (multi-agent, MR 10,000).
- **Evaluation:** Hanabi, 2-player and 3-5 player; blueprints SmartBot, WTFWThat, CloneBot, DQN (Ape-X), SAD.
- **Code:** github.com/facebookresearch/Hanabi_SPARTA; license not stated on the page
- **Fit:** partial. Measured one-step rollout gain over a learned blueprint (+0.53 points for SAD, 24.08 to 24.61), with explicit core-hour costs. It breaks two constraints: cooperative (not single-player), and it assumes the partners' policies are known exactly.

### Thinking Fast and Slow with Deep Learning and Tree Search (Expert Iteration) (2017)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/1705.08439 · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.AI); no peer-reviewed venue named · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Train a neural policy by imitating tree search, then use the policy to guide a stronger search: the search-teacher-student loop the owner wants.
- **Method:** Expert = MCTS (UCT and RAVE) with 10,000 simulations per move and uniform rollouts. Apprentice = CNN (13 conv layers, two softmax heads). Target = root visit distribution (tree-policy targets), positions from exploration policies with DAgger-style aggregation. The policy net biases UCT at search time (w_a = 100, temperature 0.1); value head optional (w_v = 0.75).
- **Assumes:** Two-player, perfect information, zero-sum, deterministic (Hex); the framework is an MDP extended by training both players; no game knowledge beyond rules; game chosen because move order does not change the result.
- **Claims:** Target comparison after the first 100,000 MCTS moves: top-1 error CAT 47.0% vs TPT 47.7%; TPT 50 +- 13 Elo stronger. Three more DAgger batches of 100,000 moves added about 120 Elo; final net won 87/162 games vs the MCTS it imitated (just over half). Policy-guided MCTS at 10,000 iterations won 97% vs baseline MCTS at 10,000. Network evaluation made search about 2x slower; doubling vanilla MCTS iterations gave a 56% win rate. Value-and-policy ExIt beat policy-only ExIt after about 550,000 positions plus about 7.4 million more moves. Vs MoHex 1.0: 75.3% at 10,000 iterations; 59.3% at 100,000 iterations; 55.6% at 4 seconds per move. Speed on Xeon E5-1620 + Titan X: 0.3 s per 10,000 iterations (ExIt) vs 0.2 s (MoHex).
- **Evaluation:** Hex 9x9, tabula rasa, 162-game matches; comparisons vs MoHex 1.0 and vs REINFORCE.
- **Code:** none announced (references MoHex source code, owned by its authors)
- **Fit:** partial. The best match for the teacher-beats-student loop with measured gains per batch of search-generated data; the final net only just beat the MCTS it imitated (87/162), which is the failure mode to watch. Perfect information, deterministic, two-player; no hidden draw order; the net is a GPU-trained CNN (Titan X) that the constraints would need to swap for a CPU-scale net.

### Multiagent Rollout Algorithms and Reinforcement Learning (Bertsekas) (2019, v3 2020)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/1910.00120 · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.LG); no venue named · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Standard rollout over joint controls of m agents costs exponentially in m; can agents choose their components one at a time and keep the improvement property?
- **Method:** Agents act in a fixed order; each runs a one-step lookahead over its own component, with earlier agents' choices fixed and later agents following the base policy. Infinite horizon: an agent-by-agent policy iteration with ties broken in favor of the current component (prevents cycling).
- **Assumes:** Finite horizon: stochastic disturbances with a known model; all agents observe the state; known predecessor choices; Cartesian constraint sets. Infinite horizon: discounted, finite states and controls, discount alpha in (0,1). Any base policy works, but an agent-by-agent optimal base cannot be strictly improved this way. Without communication of predecessors' choices, improvement can fail (Example 3.1: per-stage costs 1 base, 2 uncoordinated, 0 optimal).
- **Claims:** Cost improvement (Proposition 2.1): the multiagent rollout policy is no worse than the base at every state and stage. Per-stage complexity O(s^m) for standard rollout vs O(sm) agent-by-agent, with s the largest control-set size. Convergence (Proposition 4.1): agent-by-agent policy iteration reaches an agent-by-agent optimal policy in finitely many iterations (not necessarily globally optimal). The paper reports no benchmark numbers; the spiders-and-fly example is qualitative.
- **Evaluation:** Toy examples only (spiders and fly; two spiders and two flies).
- **Code:** none found
- **Fit:** partial. Gives the theory for why a one-step rollout improvement never loses to its base policy and how to keep that when the action is a tuple of parts (per-agent or per-unit). The constraints it meets: known model, finite states. It gives no numbers, and it does not cover hidden information.

### Learned Belief Search: Efficiently Improving Policies in Partially Observable Settings (2021)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/2106.09086 (also arXiv 2106.09086) · **Venue:** arXiv preprint (cs.AI); no venue named · **Cited by:** not checked · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** SPARTA needs a small enumerable belief space; search cost grows with the amount of hidden information.
- **Method:** Replace exact belief with an auto-regressive counterfactual belief, trained with maximum likelihood, that predicts other players' private cards one at a time. Search samples hands from that model and drops those inconsistent with the searcher's observations. A public-private architecture (blueprint LSTM on public features; MLP on private plus public, fused multiplicatively before a dueling head) avoids replaying games to rebuild hidden state. Q-values come from N-step Monte Carlo rollouts, bootstrapped with the blueprint's value; the searcher deviates only if the best action beats the blueprint's by delta = 0.05; UCB-like pruning.
- **Assumes:** Two-player Hanabi self-play, 5-card (plus 6- and 7-card variants, max hints cut to 4 for 7-card); a simulator and the other agents' blueprint policies are available at test time; only one player searches (the belief model is trained on blueprint play). Running both players gave 24.34 +- 0.02 vs 24.48 +- 0.01 for single-agent LBS.
- **Claims:** Hanabi 5-card, mean +- s.e.m. over 5,000 games: blueprint 24.08 +- 0.01 (<1 s per game); SPARTA 24.52 +- 0.01 (215 s); LBS 16-step rollouts 24.48 +- 0.01 (47 s), about 91% of SPARTA's gain at about 4.6x lower compute. 1-step rollouts: about 55% of the benefit at about 35.8x speedup (paper's claim). 6-card: blueprint 24.57; SPARTA 24.82 (1,747 s); LBS 24.76 (42 s), about 76% of the gain at about 42x less time. 7-card: blueprint 23.67; SPARTA out of memory (64 GB); LBS 24.26 (58 s). Strongest blueprint (1024 units, 72 h): blueprint 24.42; SPARTA 24.66; LBS 24.62, 83% of SPARTA's gain at 69 s vs 364 s.
- **Evaluation:** Hanabi self-play, 5/6/7-card; hardware: search 5 CPU cores, 1 GPU, 64 GB RAM.
- **Code:** none released yet; the authors say they "plan to open-source all of the code for LBS"
- **Fit:** partial. Shows how to get most of a search gain at a fraction of the CPU time when the belief is sampled, not enumerated, with the measured trade (about 91% of gain at 4.6x less time). Hanabi is cooperative and needs a GPU for the belief model; the sampling idea is the part that might carry over to hidden draw order.

### Approximate Modified Policy Iteration (AMPI) (2012)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/html/1205.3054 (v2) · **Venue:** arXiv preprint; cites the authors' INRIA tech report for the full proofs and experiments · **Cited by:** OpenAlex returned 19 in one query and 20 in another for W2123917165; not settled · **Read:** full text
- **Problem:** Modified policy iteration (m-step evaluation) for large or infinite state spaces, where evaluation and the greedy step are approximate. Does an m-step rollout-style improvement step keep its gain under error?
- **Method:** Three variants: AMPI-V (value fit from rollout estimates, greedy action estimated from M samples per action); AMPI-Q (action-value fit, exact greedy step); CBMPI (value by regression, greedy policy by cost-sensitive classification over a policy class). Error propagation tracks greedy-step error and evaluation error through Bellman-residual, distance and shift terms.
- **Assumes:** Discounted MDP (gamma in (0,1)); sample-based evaluation; policies from a set (for CBMPI, a class with finite VC dimension h); linear function approximation with bounded basis functions for the finite-sample bound; concentrability coefficients on the sampling distribution.
- **Claims:** Proposition 1: for m > 1, no norm makes the MPI update a contraction, and MPI is not monotone for finite m. Remark 2 (limit bound): lim sup_{k->inf} ||l_k||_inf <= (2 gamma sup_j ||eps_j||_inf + sup_j ||eps'_j||_inf) / (1 - gamma)^2. Theorem 2 (CBMPI): holds with probability 1 - delta, combining approximation errors and estimation errors that depend on sample sizes n, N, M and VC dimension h. Remark 6: with budget B fixed, the bound is O(gamma^m (d_m + sqrt(m/B)) + d' + sqrt(M|A|m/B)) up to constants and logs: a larger m trades approximation error against estimation error. The text gives no experiments (Appendix G of the tech report, not in this page).
- **Evaluation:** None in the page; the authors cite preliminary benchmark results in the tech report.
- **Code:** none found
- **Fit:** partial. The only theory here on how error in a rollout or evaluation step carries through repeated improvement, which is the question for a teacher-student loop where each generation is trained on the last one's search. It is a bound on error, not a measured gain, and it assumes a discounted MDP with no hidden information.
